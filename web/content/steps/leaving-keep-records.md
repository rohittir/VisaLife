#### What it is

A few records decide how easy it is to come back.

- Days outside the US. On H-1B, time outside the US of more than 24 hours does not count toward your six years. Your employer can ask for those days back in a later H-1B petition.
- Your I-94 departure record. CBP records your exit from your airline's data.
- Your visa stamp. It stays valid until its expiry date unless it is cancelled or revoked. CBP decides admission each time.

#### When

Before you go, save your documents.
After you go, check that your departure was recorded.

#### Forms and fees

- Keep your passport stamps, boarding passes, I-94 records and copies of your I-797 notices. Keep a chart of your dates.
- To see your travel history, use i94.cbp.dhs.gov and choose View Travel History. It shows ten years.
- If your departure is not recorded, ask CBP to fix it through Ask a Question or DHS TRIP. Attach proof of departure, such as your ticket, entry stamps or pay stubs.

#### What to watch for

- CBP says the travel history is a helpful tool, not an official record. It can miss land crossings.
- When your employer tells USCIS you no longer work there, your H-1B approval is revoked automatically. To bring you back, the employer needs a new petition.
- After one full year outside the US, you can start a new six-year period. A new cap-subject petition would need a cap number.
- If you stay past the date on your I-94, your visa is generally voided. A recorded departure shows you left on time.
- F-1: if your SEVIS record has ended, coming back to study means a new I-20, a new SEVIS ID and the I-901 fee again. OPT time you used is lost.
- Talk to an immigration attorney before you assume an old petition or stamp still works.
